# GrowFi — P2P-ness Compilation & Scoring

This document compiles quantitative scores and rationales derived from evidence in `GrowFi_data.md`, following `Evaluation.md`, plus hybrid analysis (`Hybrid.md`) and ethos assessment (`Ethos.md`).

## Scoring Scale (0–5)
- 0 = Absent / contrary to P2P
- 1 = Weak / token
- 2 = Partial / significant gaps
- 3 = Moderate / functional
- 4 = Strong / high fidelity
- 5 = Exemplary / benchmark

## Interpretation Rule: Profile Before Score

GrowFi should be read as a dynamic P2P profile, not reduced to a single total. The optional index below is useful for comparison, but the main finding is the pattern: strong protocol transparency and commons software posture, weaker organizational governance, missing contributor accounting, and incomplete evidence on lived experience, capital governance, and path-dependency safeguards.

## Dynamic Profile and Averages
- Structural average: 2.25
- Operational average: 2.15
- Economic average: 2.25
- Cultural/Ecosystem average: 2.50
- Overall P2P-ness index (optional, simple avg of layer avgs): 2.29
- Strongest layer(s): Cultural/Ecosystem, mostly because mission coherence and engineering learning are visible.
- Weakest layer(s): Operational/Process, mainly because coordination, contributor accounting, conflict governance, reputation, and social accountability are thinly documented.
- Trajectory: early transitional hybrid with meaningful P2P technical/protocol elements and unresolved governance/social-layer centralization risks.
- Main tensions:
  - Openness vs quality: permissionless campaign narrative coexists with owner/factory-gated setup and unclear campaign approval criteria.
  - Transparency vs privacy: protocol transparency is strong, but privacy-preserving transparency boundaries are not documented.
  - Meritocracy/reputation vs incumbent capture: no social reputation system is visible, while technical/admin authority may concentrate around early builders and owners.
  - Capital access vs commons governance: campaign-level capital flows are explicit, but organization-level capital influence and treasury governance are unclear.
  - Formal decentralization vs lived agency: onchain mechanisms reduce discretion, but contributor and grower lived experience is not yet evidenced.

## Scenario / Simulation Recommendations
- Contribution accounting scenarios: model code, protocol design, grower onboarding, dMRV, community support, legal/admin, and care/coordination contributions; test whether non-code work remains invisible.
- Governance scenarios: test owner exit, multisig transition, campaign approval disputes, emergency powers, fork attempts, and low-participation governance.
- Benefit redistribution scenarios: test failed campaigns, delayed harvests, liquidity shocks, high redemption demand, fee-use disputes, and treasury shortfalls.
- Reputation scenarios: model grower reliability, campaign performance history, contributor reputation, appeals, and recovery after failed delivery.
- Highest-priority simulation/backtest: owner/factory governance transition plus campaign approval/appeal process, because it directly affects the gap between permissionless framing and actual access.

## Hybridization X-ray (per `Hybrid.md`)
- Legal existence/entity: Hybrid; mission-linked host association is named, but full legal architecture is not fully documented in public sources.
- Ownership of IP/outputs: P2P-leaning for software (MIT), unknown for trademarks/brand and non-code assets.
- Capital structure/funding: Hybrid/emergent; campaign-level public crowdfund model is clear, protocol org-level funding stack is opaque.
- Decision-making: Hybrid; smart-contract state machine minimizes discretionary admin in campaigns, but factory owner-gated actions remain.
- Formal vs emergent authority: Mixed; protocol enforces formal actor roles; little evidence of emergent peer governance.
- Contribution accounting & rewards: Strong for onchain participant economics; weak for non-code/community contributor accounting.
- Revenue model: Market-facing and transparent at protocol layer (2% fee), limited enterprise financial transparency.
- Infrastructure control: Open code and public contracts/subgraph claims; infrastructure concentration details not yet assessed.
- Roadmap ownership: Not clearly documented in reviewed sources.
- Trademark/brand control: Not documented in reviewed sources.
- Privacy-preserving transparency: Not documented; protocol transparency is visible, but privacy boundaries are not specified.
- Capital subordination to commons governance: Not evidenced at organization level; campaign mechanics are transparent, but treasury/funder influence is unclear.
- Reputation/meritocracy capture risk: Social reputation system not found; practical authority may concentrate through owner/admin, maintainer, and deployment knowledge.

Red flags to check:
- Missing explicit trademark/fork naming policy.
- Unknown governance rights outside contract execution.
- Unknown contributor-recognition framework for non-token participants.
- Persistent owner/factory powers without public sunset, review, or appeal process.
- Missing budget/treasury and capital-control disclosures.
- Missing participant lived-experience evidence.

## Conclusion
- GrowFi currently shows a technically transparent, mechanism-centric profile with commons-friendly software licensing, explicit onchain economic logic, and a coherent regenerative-finance mission.
- Its strongest P2P elements are protocol verifiability, deterministic escrow/redemption mechanics, public MIT-licensed code, and early engineering learning loops.
- Its weakest areas are organizational governance, contributor accounting, privacy-preserving transparency, capital-governance visibility, conflict/fork governance, and direct evidence of participant lived experience.
- The central complexity risk is path-dependent authority: owner/factory controls, early technical authority, unclear treasury/funder influence, and infrastructure dependencies could become capture points if they are not made reviewable and accountable.
- The profile is best described as an early transitional hybrid: strong P2P aspirations and protocol-level openness, but not yet a mature peer-governed commons ecosystem.
